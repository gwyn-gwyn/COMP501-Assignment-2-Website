Technology Opportunities

Introduction

Telesurgery “began with early telepresence systems in the late twentieth century, aimed at providing surgical expertise across distances” (Godley et al., 2025, p. 1). The field expanded in the early 2000s, most notably marked by the Lindbergh Operation in 2001, introducing transatlantic telesurgery with low latency using ATM.

The Beginning

Opportunities presented then began showing proof of concept even in rural areas, first establishing the World’s First Telerobotic Remote Surgical Service in Canada (Anvari et al., 2005), which used commercial fiberoptic network (IP-VN) and an existing ZEUS robot between rural and teaching hospitals. This demonstrated the potential to extend surgical care to other rural sites with minimal expense. This initiative was discontinued in 2005 due to technological constraints and high infrastructure costs following the merger of Computer Motion with Intuitive Surgical. Nevertheless, the findings laid the groundwork for exploring advanced long-distance procedures such as urology, neurovascular interventions and robotic stroke clot removals among others.

Recent Advancements

Building on these foundational efforts, recent studies have demonstrated an unprecedented range. For instance, Moschovas et al. (2025) explored expanding telesurgery to the African continent following experiments where “surgeons in Orlando operated on live porcine models in Shanghai, using telesurgery consoles to perform urologic procedures” (p. 1) across the longest recorded distances between Orlando, Florida, USA, and Shanghai, China (approximately 13,000 km). Though a technological proof-of-concept where widespread adoption still requires resolving international legal liability and ethical framework disparities between nations, the authors emphasize the need to “drive policy changes that make telesurgery and other technologies sustainable” (p. 3).

Particularly regarding the steep learning curve and a contributor to high costs, a “physical prototype and virtual simulation system” was developed featuring three control modes: GUI, joystick and color-based sorting for two serial manipulator arms, “offering flexible training for surgeons” (Vidyasagar et al., 2024, p. 1). It provides a framework where the “simulation environment allows surgeons to refine their skills and tackle complex procedures, while the 4-DOF prototype offers hands-on training for various control methods and applications” (p. 4) that previously limited access to smaller hospitals and created disparities in the surgeon training and experience.

Possible Drawbacks

Furthermore, the concerns of high costs and technological constraints have been addressed with several articles detailing how the field plans to combat them as technology has advanced with the use of AI (Godley et al., 2025, p. 5) and high-speed 5G and fiber-optic networks (Moschovas et al., 2025, p. 1). Advances have also been modeled by Ouda et al. (2026) by proposing a Satellite-Driven Digital Twin Approach (see Figure 1) that has been tested in simulation. While 5G would work in urban areas with dense populations that justify “small cell installations,” rural areas lack” financial incentives for operators and ... infrastructure for 5G’s high-frequency spectrum.” To combat this, LEO (Low Earth Orbit) satellite networks offer a scalable solution that “can provide the connectivity needed to synchronize physical surgical systems with their digital twins” (p. 1). Though not without challenges, atmospheric interference and cross-border network regulation pose risks to the stability of this method. Still, researchers have begun proposing “context-aware management strategies where systems adapt to predicted disruptions” such as monitoring orbital parameters (pp. 1, 4) with causal forecasting to give surgeons enough time to react and adapt.

Conclusion

Ultimately, the simulated scenarios present a feasible opportunity to optimize and specifically target the purpose of reaching geographically isolated populations where specialized healthcare is otherwise unavailable. Other critical areas include conflict zones where “patients will benefit from ... reduced travel, better‐qualified surgeon services, ... resulting in reduced costs and increased hospital efficiency” (Rahmah & Shariat, 2024 , p. 2). It also reduces human interactions, helping reduce contamination and infection rates.

In recent years, this transition has become evident from theoretical opportunities to tangible futures “offering a pathway toward a more equitable healthcare system where access to advanced medical interventions is no longer confined to urban centers” (Moschovas et al., 2025, p. 2).

In summary, ongoing advancements in simulation, low-latency connectivity, and adaptive networks are systematically overcoming historical cost and technical hurdles, transforming telesurgery from a conceptual goal into a practical reality.
