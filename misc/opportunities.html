Technology Opportunities

Introduction

Telesurgery “began with early telepresence systems in the late twentieth century, aimed at providing surgical expertise across distances” (Godley et al., 2025, p. 1). The field expanded in the early 2000s, most notably marked by the Lindbergh Operation in 2001, introducing transatlantic telesurgery with low latency using ATM.

The Beginning

Opportunities soon began showing proof of concept in rural areas, first establishing the World’s First Telerobotic Remote Surgical Service in Canada (Anvari et al., 2005), using a commercial fiberoptic network (IP-VN) and an existing ZEUS robot between rural and teaching hospitals. This demonstrated the potential to extend surgical care to other rural sites with minimal expense. The findings laid the groundwork for exploring advanced long-distance procedures such as urology and neurovascular interventions among others.

Recent Advancements

Building further, recent studies have demonstrated an unprecedented range. For instance, Moschovas et al. (2025) explored expanding telesurgery to the African continent following experiments where “surgeons in Orlando operated on live porcine models in Shanghai, using telesurgery consoles to perform urologic procedures” (p. 1) across the longest recorded distance of approximately 13,000 km. While an impressive proof-of-concept, international legal and ethical disparities remain. However, the authors do emphasize the need to “drive policy changes that make telesurgery and other technologies sustainable” (p. 3).

To address high costs and the steep learning curve associated with training, Vidyasagar et al. (2024, p. 1) developed a “physical prototype and virtual simulation system” featuring three control modes (GUI, joystick, and color-based sorting) for two manipulator arms, “offering flexible training for surgeons.” Their platform provides a framework where the “simulation environment allows surgeons to refine their skills and tackle complex procedures” (p. 4), directly helping smaller hospitals overcome historical differences in surgeon training and experience.

Addressing Technical & Network Constraints

Researchers address possible drawbacks such as cost and network constraints using AI (Godley et al., 2025, p. 5), high-speed 5G, fiber-optic networks, and satellite digital twins (Moschovas et al., 2025; Ouda et al., 2026). Because rural areas lack the "financial incentives... and... infrastructure for 5G’s high-frequency spectrum," Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites offer a scalable solution to "synchronize physical surgical systems with their digital twins" (Ouda et al., 2026, p. 1). Although currently tested only in simulation, this satellite approach lowers the barrier of access for rural regions. To counter atmospheric disruptions, systems utilize "context-aware management strategies" and causal forecasting to give surgeons time to adapt (Ouda et al., 2026, pp. 1, 4).

Conclusion & Local Application

Ultimately, these technological advancements and simulated scenarios offer a feasible path to optimize telesurgery for its primary purpose: reaching geographically isolated populations. This includes critical settings such as conflict zones (Rahmah & Shariat, 2024, p. 2) and rural regions in New Zealand. As Gurney et al. (2024) highlight, significant travel equity disparities exist for Māori in NZ requiring surgical care for conditions like lung cancer. Furthermore, given the “stark shortage of general surgeons in rural areas” and distance remaining a “significant barrier to surgical care” (Cowan et al., 2025, p. 8), telesurgery could provide the breakthrough NZ needs to bridge these healthcare gaps.

In summary, ongoing advancements in simulation, low-latency connectivity, and adaptive networks are systematically overcoming historical cost and technical hurdles, transforming telesurgery from a conceptual goal and opportunity into a practical reality.
