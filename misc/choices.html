Technology Choices

Group Choices

After considering robotics and prosthetics, our group unanimously chose telesurgery due to our shared interest in robotics, networks, and healthcare. Telesurgery is promoted to deliver complex care to distant patients, but it relies on expensive robotics, low-latency networks, and skilled local staff (Dohler et al., 2024; Saikali et al., 2024); resources rural regions often lack. In Aotearoa, only 14.3% of New Zealand general surgeons work rurally, although nearly half the population lives outside the main centers (Cowan et al., 2025). Māori travels a median of 57 km to reach lung cancer surgery, compared with 34 km for Europeans (Gurney et al., 2024). Rather than exploring robotics or prosthetics broadly, we asked a sharper question about who benefits and who is left out.

Our focus comprised socio-economic impacts rather than technical feasibility. The trade-off is that real-world use is still limited. A systematic review found only eight human studies of remote telesurgery between 2001 and 2020 (Barba et al., 2022). Much of our evidence comes from trials and case studies rather than established systems.

Design and Implementation Choices

How telesurgery develops depends on several choices, each with a trade-off.

Autonomy

AI assistance could reduce surgeon workload through real-time analytics and help with instrument control (Godley et al., 2025). But AI assistance raises the question of liability. Even when an autonomous system causes harm, blame still tends to fall on the surgeon (Jamjoom et al., 2022; Godley et al., 2025). Fully autonomous surgery would no longer be considered telesurgery since a surgeon will no longer be remotely performing the surgery. We think surgeon-controlled systems with AI support are the realistic path, because the technology has not yet been shown outside the lab, and nobody has settled who is accountable when it fails (Kim et al. 2025).

Network

Latency is the main constraint. Motiwala et al. (2025) identified under 200 ms as ideal to use the surgical machine. The Lindbergh Operation worked at 155 ms between New York and France (Marescaux et al., 2002). With dedicated fiber, 5G and eventually 6G links can keep delay low enough to perform such surgeries precisely (Dohler et al., 2024), but this kind of technology heavily favors places that already have the infrastructure set up. Cheaper public connections would reach more places however it risks delays that affect surgical performance (Xu et al., 2014).

Ownership and deployment

A public hub-and-spoke model as used in the first telerobotic service serving a rural Canadian community (Anvari et al., 2005), could spread specialist access more fairly. Private proprietary systems may drive innovation but concentrate care where hospitals can afford it.

Regulations

Regulations are a major factor in cross-border telesurgery due to differing international healthcare laws. The Billing, training, and liability rules differ across borders (Elendu et al., 2025; Al Sabah & Al Haddad, 2026). Japan's guidelines show that a national framework is possible (Mori et al., 2024), but requires persistent effort and negotiations. A shared framework would make cross-border care workable, but it would take years to agree.
