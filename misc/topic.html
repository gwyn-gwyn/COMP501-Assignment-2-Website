Chosen Technology/Topic

Our chosen topic is remote surgeries (also known as telesurgery), arising from our shared interest in robotics, networks, and healthcare. We specifically focus on how it intends to provide access to medical care for people in underdeveloped regions, low-income countries, conflict zones, or rural areas where doctors and surgeons aren't readily available due to safety risks and travel costs (Rahmah & Shariat, 2024).

The transcontinental capabilities of this technology were proven feasible in 2001 with the Lindbergh Operation, where surgeons in New York successfully operated on a patient in Strasbourg, France, 6,300 km away (Marescaux et al., 2002). However, analyzing telesurgery across multiple levels of social analysis reveals significant ethical implications:

  • Institutional & Global Level (Equity & Access): Telesurgeries depend on expensive robotics, high-speed low-latency networks, and skilled local staff to operate equipment (Dohler et al., 2024).

  • Individual & Professional Level (Responsibility & Reliability): It introduces complexities in the doctor-patient relationship regarding liability laws and billing accountability should adverse outcomes arise (Elendu et al., 2025).

This raises the significant question: does telesurgery actually close the healthcare gap, or does it widen it by concentrating advanced care wherever infrastructure already exists?

As we explore the opportunities, risks, and choices embedded in this topic as it continues to evolve, we discover the implications it has on:

  • Jobs: Both streamlining and complicating the process of specializing.

  • Healthcare: With potential to expand access to surgical expertise.

  • Costs: Reducing travel and training costs when disregarding the expense on technology.

All have grave impacts on the medical field, exposing whether the technology truly fulfills the geographic accessibility it was designed for, or is another perk of the privileged, fueling infrastructural inequality.
